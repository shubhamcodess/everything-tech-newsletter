## [45] Researchers use AI to design proteins that cut up other proteins
Ars Technica | full text via Ars Technica | ~1743 words

A protein is a chain of amino acids held together by what are called peptide bonds. On their own, those bonds are extremely stable; they’re estimated to have a half-life of several hundred years. But in Earth’s environment, most proteins don’t last nearly that long. That’s because organisms have evolved a handful of ways to catalyze the break-up of peptide bonds, which have diversified into countless specialized protein-digesting enzymes. Proteases are everywhere and generally make short work of any proteins that find their way into the environment.
Now, researchers have basically short-circuited the diversification process, using AI-based protein design tools to take the catalytic core of two types of protease and replace the entire rest of the protein. The resulting enzymes could be tuned to cut specific proteins in half, and the researchers demonstrated their effectiveness by using them to cut proteins associated with neurological diseases.
Cutting proteins
The basic peptide bond isn’t especially interesting-looking—it’s what you get when you link an amino group to an acid, releasing a water molecule in the process. As their name implies, amino acids have both an amino and an acid group, allowing them to form peptide bonds on two sides of the amino acid. This is critical for building up the long chains found in proteins. (The amino acids also have side chains with distinctive chemistry, which gives a backbone specific properties.)

Breaking these bonds apart simply reverses the process by breaking apart the peptide bond and a water molecule, and linking their parts. Because both components are chemically stable, the reaction requires an enzyme to occur with any frequency. Without an enzyme, a typical peptide bond would be stable for hundreds of years in water.
Life has evolved a handful of enzyme mechanisms that manage this catalysis. The research at issue here relied on two different ones. One is a zinc protease, where the positive charge of the zinc atom coordinates the peptide bond that’s being broken, key parts of the protease, and a water molecule. Our new repo star history API now provides trends without returning individual identities or requiring unbounded database work. These are the engineering tradeoffs we made.
Maintainers look at star history to answer a practical question: did a launch, release, or event change a project’s trajectory? To reconstruct that curve from the GitHub API, tools had to request pages of stargazer records (each person who currently stars the repository and when they starred it), keep the timestamps, and throw the identities away.
Over time, that design created problems at both ends. Identities were available for bulk collection, so bad actors started scraping public stargazer lists for spam and phishing campaigns. For highly starred repositories, the endpoint could also make the database process many thousands of entries to return a single page.
The fix seemed clear: return counts instead of people. But returning less data didn’t actually make the query any cheaper.
Now maintainers have a direct way to get the curve: we built an endpoint that returns up to 30 weeks of daily star counts in a single request, with no identities attached. Keeping that cheap to serve meant reducing how much data the database processed, not just how much it returned, and the endpoint does that by filtering stars by time before it counts anything.
What maintainers need from star history
We first addressed the immediate risks to developers and to our database by restricting stargazer and watcher lists to repository administrators and collaborators. After the access change, callers without the required permission could receive an empty response or a 403 Forbidden response.
That change also cut off tools that had used the old response to calculate star history, often without displaying or otherwise using the usernames it returned. So we talked with maintainers and tool builders to understand what they still needed. They used star history as an approximate signal of interest: to compare activity before and after a release, or to see whether an announcement reached new developers. Tackling a shared-memory bottleneck
Specifically, the fix addresses contention in Linux’s page cache, which holds file data in memory. When several threads repeatedly read small amounts of data from the same cached file, they take and release references to the folio backing that data. Those reference-count updates require atomic writes to a shared cache line. This in turn forces processor cores to compete over it.
